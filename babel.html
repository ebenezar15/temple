III

Part three

Babel, Babylon and the City of God

Humanity keeps trying to build its own mountain of God: a city, a tower and a name, with a god kept on retainer. Scripture answers with a city God designs and builds himself.

Genesis 11:1–9

The tower everyone pictures

Pieter Bruegel the Elder’s 1563 painting is the image most of us carry: a spiralling, cloud-piercing tower swarming with workers. The usual moral follows: proud people tried to climb up to God, so God scrambled their languages.

Ancient readers would have seen something else. The text gives a location (Shinar, southern Mesopotamia), a building method (fired brick and bitumen) and a phrase (“its top in the heavens”) that together point to one kind of building: a ziggurat, a temple-tower.

The land of Shinar and beyond

Map of the story

Turn on layers to trace the rivers of Eden, the cities of Babel, Abraham’s call out of Ur, the exile to Babylon, and the nations gathered at Pentecost.

Anatomy of a temple-tower

What is a ziggurat?

A ziggurat was not a ladder for people to climb up to heaven. It was a man-made holy mountain, built so that a god would come down, live in the shrine at its top, and bless the city below. Build one, then watch what Genesis 11:5 does to it.

Names carved in brick

Ziggurat names describe what the building was for, not how tall it was.

“Its top in the heavens”, to scale

Babylon’s great ziggurat, Etemenanki, stood about 91 m tall. Here it is beside other tall structures.

What actually went wrong

Three layers of Babel’s rebellion

A Hebrew wordplay

“Let us make a שֵׁם shem for ourselves”

The word for “name” is shem, which is also the name of Noah’s son. Babel tries to build a name out of brick. The very next paragraph is “the generations of Shem” (Gen 11:10), a family line God builds out of people, ending in Abram, to whom God says: “I will make your name great” (Gen 12:2). Trace the line.

“Come, let us…”: God answers in the same words

The name that is given

Babel: “Let us make a name for ourselves” Gen 11:4

Abram: “I will make your name great, so that you will be a blessing … in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed” Gen 12:2–3

Pentecost: “Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ” Acts 2:38

Exalted: “God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name” Phil 2:9

Deuteronomy 32:8–9 · the Table of Nations

Seventy nations and one portion

Genesis 10 lists about seventy nations descended from Noah’s sons. Deuteronomy 32 remembers Babel this way: “When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance, when he divided mankind, he fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God. But the LORD’s portion is his people, Jacob his allotted heritage.”

The reading “sons of God” comes from the Dead Sea Scrolls (4QDeutj) and is reflected in the Greek Septuagint; the later Masoretic text reads “sons of Israel.” Many scholars, including Michael Heiser, take the older reading to mean God placed the scattered nations under lesser spiritual beings, who later demanded worship as the “gods of the nations” (Deut 4:19–20; Ps 82), while he kept one people for himself, a nation that did not exist yet.

Then, in the very next chapter, he calls Abram.

Two gates

“Gate of the gods” and “the gate of heaven”

Babylon’s own name, Bāb-ilim, means “gate of the god(s)”. Genesis gives the true gate in the next generations, and it is not built by anyone.

Babylon

𒆍𒀭𒊏𒆠 · Bāb-ilim

“Gate of the gods”. A city built around a ziggurat whose stairway was meant to bring a god down to his shrine on human terms.

Bethel · Genesis 28:12–17

A stairway Jacob did not build

Fleeing, alone, with a stone for a pillow, Jacob dreams of a sullam (a stairway, likely evoking a ziggurat ramp) set on earth with its top reaching heaven, angels going up and down, and the LORD standing above it. God speaks promises unasked. Jacob wakes: “This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven.”

John 1:51

The stairway is a person

Jesus to Nathanael: “You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man.” The meeting place of heaven and earth is Jesus himself.

Genesis 11 ↔ Acts 2

Pentecost: Babel in reverse

Drag the divider. At Babel, people build up so a god will come down, and God comes down to scatter them. At Pentecost, people only wait, and God comes down to gather them.

Babel · Genesis 11

Many scattered

  • Humans take the initiative: “Come, let us build.”
  • A ziggurat is built to summon a god on the city’s terms.
  • God comes down to confuse.
  • One language becomes many; understanding collapses.
  • The people are scattered over the earth.
  • “Let us make a name for ourselves.”

Pentecost · Acts 2

Scattered gathered

  • God takes the initiative; the disciples simply wait (Acts 1:4).
  • No shrine and no ritual: an upper room.
  • The Spirit comes down as wind and divided tongues of fire.
  • Many languages remain, yet “each one was hearing them speak in his own language” (Acts 2:6).
  • Devout people “from every nation under heaven” are gathered (Acts 2:5).
  • “…in the name of Jesus Christ” (Acts 2:38).

A tale of two cities

The city of man and the city of God

From Cain’s first city to “Babylon the great”, people build cities to secure themselves without God. Running alongside is another city that Abraham waited for, “the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God” (Heb 11:10). Select a city.

“Two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self.”Augustine of Hippo, The City of God XIV.28 (AD 426)

Babylon’s counterfeit EdenBabylon offered its own version of every Eden image: an artificial mountain (Etemenanki), a gate of the gods (the blue-glazed Ishtar Gate on the Processional Way) and, according to the later writer Berossus, terraced “hanging gardens” Nebuchadnezzar built because his wife missed her green mountains. “Is not this great Babylon, which I have built … by my mighty power … for the glory of my majesty?” (Dan 4:30).
