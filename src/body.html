Cosmic geography

The mountain of God, in three rings

In the ancient Near East the gods lived on a cosmic mountain, the high centre of the world where life-giving water flowed out and heaven touched earth. Eden, Sinai and the Tabernacle share the same three zones of holiness. Switch between them.

East means away from GodExiles from God’s presence keep moving east: Adam and Eve are driven out on the east (Gen 3:24), Cain settles “east of Eden” (Gen 4:16), Babel’s builders migrate east to Shinar (Gen 11:2), and Lot chooses the east toward Sodom (Gen 13:11). The Tabernacle and Temple face east, so every worshipper walks west, back toward the presence.

Man in the sanctuary

Adam: image, priest and king

Ancient kings set up statues of themselves to mark the lands they ruled, and temples were not finished until the god’s image was installed. Genesis says God placed his own living image, humanity, inside his garden-temple to serve, guard and rule.

Tree of life → lampstand

A golden tree in the holy place

The menorah was hammered from one piece of pure gold, with branches, almond blossoms, buds and flowers (Exod 25:31–40). It stood just outside the veil like a stylised tree of life, burning in the presence of God.

Aaron’s staff, kept beside the Ark, “sprouted and put forth buds and produced blossoms, and it bore ripe almonds” (Num 17:8). Life springs up again near the throne.

In Revelation 1:12–20 the risen Christ walks among seven golden lampstands, his churches. In Revelation 22:2 the tree of life stands in the city again.

Part two

The Serpent and the Seed

Who, or what, spoke to Eve in God’s garden? Read Genesis 3 slowly, in Hebrew and beside Ezekiel and Isaiah, and the “snake” turns out to be a shining throne-guardian who rebelled. His curse contains the Bible’s first promise of a rescuer.

Genesis 3:1 · one Hebrew word

Nāḥāš: serpent, diviner, shining one

English Bibles say “the serpent”. The Hebrew word נָחָשׁ sits in a cluster of words that also mean “to divine” and “bronze”. Choose a meaning, then see what they describe together.

Reading Genesis 3 carefully

Six things a garden snake can’t do

Naked and crafty: a wordplayGenesis 2:25 ends, “the man and his wife were both naked (ʿărûmmîm) and were not ashamed.” The next verse begins, “Now the serpent was more crafty (ʿārûm)…”. Innocence meets cunning in two almost identical words. After eating, “they knew that they were naked (ʿêrummîm)” (Gen 3:7).

Ezekiel 28:12–19

The guardian cherub who fell

Ezekiel’s lament over the king of Tyre suddenly describes someone who was in Eden, on God’s mountain, covered in priestly jewels. Many readers, ancient and modern, see an older cosmic story behind the human king. Step through the lines.

The high priest’s breastpiece · Exod 28:17–20

Lit: the nine stones named in Ezekiel 28:13. Dim: the three not listed.

Isaiah 14:12–15

“Day Star, son of Dawn”

Isaiah taunts the king of Babylon as Hêlēl ben Šāḥar, “Shining one, son of Dawn”: the planet Venus, brightest in the pre-dawn sky, climbing high and then vanishing as the sun rises. Latin translated it lucifer, “light-bearer”. Press through the five “I will”s.

    “But you are brought down to Sheol, to the far reaches of the pit.” Isa 14:15

    Two paths: grasping up, or stooping down

    The Day Star tries to climb to God’s throne and falls. Christ “did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself … to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted him” (Phil 2:6–9). He, not the rebel, is “the bright morning star” (Rev 22:16).

    Throne guardians

    Seraphim: the burning serpents

    In Isaiah 6 the beings around God’s throne are śərāphîm, usually “burning ones”. But śārāph is also a word for a serpent. A serpentine guardian at the throne was a familiar idea to ancient readers, which makes the nāḥāš in God’s garden less strange.

    Isaiah 6:2

    Above the throne

    “Above him stood the seraphim. Each had six wings: with two he covered his face, and with two he covered his feet, and with two he flew.”

    Numbers 21:6

    Fiery serpents

    “The LORD sent fiery serpents (hannəḥāšîm haśśərāphîm) among the people.” Both words, side by side.

    Isaiah 14:29; 30:6

    Flying seraph

    Isaiah twice mentions “a flying fiery serpent” (śārāph məʿôpēph): a winged serpent.

    Egypt

    The uraeus

    Egyptian royal and divine images were guarded by the rearing, often winged, cobra. It is the regional counterpart of the cherub, the hybrid guardian of Mesopotamia and Phoenicia.

    Genesis 3:14–15

    The curse, decoded

    Read as zoology, the curse misfires. Read as a fall in status, from God’s mountain to the dust of death, it makes sense, and it ends with a promise. Tap each line, then follow the serpent’s long way down through Scripture.

    Zeraʿ · “offspring, seed”

    The war of the two seeds

    Genesis 3:15 sets two lines against each other. The woman’s seed runs through Seth, Noah, Abraham, Judah and David to one person, while the serpent strikes again and again to cut it off. Press play, or choose any moment.

    The woman’s seedThe serpent strikes

    Numbers 21:4–9 · John 3:14–15

    Look and live

    In the wilderness God sends fiery serpents as judgment. Moses intercedes, and God tells him to make a serpent and set it on a pole: “if a serpent bit anyone, he would look at the bronze serpent and live.” It was a nəḥaš nəḥōšet, a shining serpent.

    These serpents are God’s instruments, not the rebel of Eden, so the two should not be merged. Yet Jesus takes up the image: “As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.” The symbol of death and deception becomes the sign of healing, because he was “made to be sin” for us (2 Cor 5:21).

    Centuries later Israel was burning incense to the bronze serpent, and King Hezekiah smashed it. He called it Nehushtan (2 Kgs 18:4), a pun on nāḥāš and nəḥōšet: “just a bronze snake thing.” A gift of healing had become an idol.

    Revelation 12:9 · 20:2

    “That ancient serpent”

    Revelation names him outright: “the great dragon … that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world.” He is not a new character. He is the one from the garden, and the whole Bible traces his defeat.

    Psalm 74:13–14

    Leviathan’s heads crushed

    “You broke the heads of the sea monsters on the waters. You crushed the heads of Leviathan.” Chaos-serpent imagery, with heads crushed, as in Genesis 3:15.

    Isaiah 27:1

    The twisting serpent

    “In that day the LORD with his hard and great and strong sword will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent (nāḥāš), Leviathan the twisting serpent.”

    Hebrews 2:14 · 1 John 3:8

    Destroyed by the seed

    He shared our flesh “that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil.” “The reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the works of the devil.”

    “He shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel.” The first promise in the Bible is spoken to the serpent, inside his own curse.Genesis 3:15

    Part three

    Babel, Babylon and the City of God

    Humanity keeps trying to build its own mountain of God: a city, a tower and a name, with a god kept on retainer. Scripture answers with a city God designs and builds himself.

    Genesis 11:1–9

    The tower everyone pictures

    Pieter Bruegel the Elder’s 1563 painting is the image most of us carry: a spiralling, cloud-piercing tower swarming with workers. The usual moral follows: proud people tried to climb up to God, so God scrambled their languages.

    Ancient readers would have seen something else. The text gives a location (Shinar, southern Mesopotamia), a building method (fired brick and bitumen) and a phrase (“its top in the heavens”) that together point to one kind of building: a ziggurat, a temple-tower.

    The land of Shinar and beyond

    Map of the story

    Turn on layers to trace the rivers of Eden, the cities of Babel, Abraham’s call out of Ur, the exile to Babylon, and the nations gathered at Pentecost.

    Anatomy of a temple-tower

    What is a ziggurat?

    A ziggurat was not a ladder for people to climb up to heaven. It was a man-made holy mountain, built so that a god would come down, live in the shrine at its top, and bless the city below. Build one, then watch what Genesis 11:5 does to it.

    Names carved in brick

    Ziggurat names describe what the building was for, not how tall it was.

    “Its top in the heavens”, to scale

    Babylon’s great ziggurat, Etemenanki, stood about 91 m tall. Here it is beside other tall structures.

    What actually went wrong

    Three layers of Babel’s rebellion

    A Hebrew wordplay

    “Let us make a שֵׁם shem for ourselves”

    The word for “name” is shem, which is also the name of Noah’s son. Babel tries to build a name out of brick. The very next paragraph is “the generations of Shem” (Gen 11:10), a family line God builds out of people, ending in Abram, to whom God says: “I will make your name great” (Gen 12:2). Trace the line.

    “Come, let us…”: God answers in the same words

    The name that is given

    Babel: “Let us make a name for ourselves” Gen 11:4

    Abram: “I will make your name great, so that you will be a blessing … in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed” Gen 12:2–3

    Pentecost: “Repent and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ” Acts 2:38

    Exalted: “God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name” Phil 2:9

    Deuteronomy 32:8–9 · the Table of Nations

    Seventy nations and one portion

    Genesis 10 lists about seventy nations descended from Noah’s sons. Deuteronomy 32 remembers Babel this way: “When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance, when he divided mankind, he fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God. But the LORD’s portion is his people, Jacob his allotted heritage.”

    The reading “sons of God” comes from the Dead Sea Scrolls (4QDeutj) and is reflected in the Greek Septuagint; the later Masoretic text reads “sons of Israel.” Many scholars, including Michael Heiser, take the older reading to mean God placed the scattered nations under lesser spiritual beings, who later demanded worship as the “gods of the nations” (Deut 4:19–20; Ps 82), while he kept one people for himself, a nation that did not exist yet.

    Then, in the very next chapter, he calls Abram.

    Two gates

    “Gate of the gods” and “the gate of heaven”

    Babylon’s own name, Bāb-ilim, means “gate of the god(s)”. Genesis gives the true gate in the next generations, and it is not built by anyone.

    Babylon

    𒆍𒀭𒊏𒆠 · Bāb-ilim

    “Gate of the gods”. A city built around a ziggurat whose stairway was meant to bring a god down to his shrine on human terms.

    Bethel · Genesis 28:12–17

    A stairway Jacob did not build

    Fleeing, alone, with a stone for a pillow, Jacob dreams of a sullam (a stairway, likely evoking a ziggurat ramp) set on earth with its top reaching heaven, angels going up and down, and the LORD standing above it. God speaks promises unasked. Jacob wakes: “This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven.”

    John 1:51

    The stairway is a person

    Jesus to Nathanael: “You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man.” The meeting place of heaven and earth is Jesus himself.

    Genesis 11 ↔ Acts 2

    Pentecost: Babel in reverse

    Drag the divider. At Babel, people build up so a god will come down, and God comes down to scatter them. At Pentecost, people only wait, and God comes down to gather them.

    • God comes down to confuse.

    Pentecost · Acts 2

    Scattered gathered

    • God takes the initiative; the disciples simply wait (Acts 1:4).
    • No shrine and no ritual: an upper room.
    • The Spirit comes down as wind and divided tongues of fire.
    • Many languages remain, yet “each one was hearing them speak in his own language” (Acts 2:6).
    • Devout people “from every nation under heaven” are gathered (Acts 2:5).
    • “…in the name of Jesus Christ” (Acts 2:38).

    “We hear them telling in our own tongues the mighty works of God.” Diversity is not erased; the barrier is.

    A tale of two cities

    The city of man and the city of God

    From Cain’s first city to “Babylon the great”, people build cities to secure themselves without God. Running alongside is another city that Abraham waited for, “the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God” (Heb 11:10). Select a city.

    “Two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self.”Augustine of Hippo, The City of God XIV.28 (AD 426)

    Revelation 17–18 ↔ 21–22

    Babylon the great, or the New Jerusalem

    John sees two women who are two cities, and the reader is invited to leave one and enter the other.

    ThemeBabylonNew Jerusalem
    Babylon’s counterfeit EdenBabylon offered its own version of every Eden image: an artificial mountain (Etemenanki), a gate of the gods (the blue-glazed Ishtar Gate on the Processional Way) and, according to the later writer Berossus, terraced “hanging gardens” Nebuchadnezzar built because his wife missed her green mountains. “Is not this great Babylon, which I have built … by my mighty power … for the glory of my majesty?” (Dan 4:30).
