Part two

The Serpent and the Seed

Who, or what, spoke to Eve in God’s garden? Read Genesis 3 slowly, in Hebrew and beside Ezekiel and Isaiah, and the “snake” turns out to be a shining throne-guardian who rebelled. His curse contains the Bible’s first promise of a rescuer.

Genesis 3:1 · one Hebrew word

Nāḥāš: serpent, diviner, shining one

English Bibles say “the serpent”. The Hebrew word נָחָשׁ sits in a cluster of words that also mean “to divine” and “bronze”. Choose a meaning, then see what they describe together.

Reading Genesis 3 carefully

Six things a garden snake can’t do

Naked and crafty: a wordplayGenesis 2:25 ends, “the man and his wife were both naked (ʿărûmmîm) and were not ashamed.” The next verse begins, “Now the serpent was more crafty (ʿārûm)…”. Innocence meets cunning in two almost identical words. After eating, “they knew that they were naked (ʿêrummîm)” (Gen 3:7).

Ezekiel 28:12–19

The guardian cherub who fell

Ezekiel’s lament over the king of Tyre suddenly describes someone who was in Eden, on God’s mountain, covered in priestly jewels. Many readers, ancient and modern, see an older cosmic story behind the human king. Step through the lines.

The high priest’s breastpiece · Exod 28:17–20

Lit: the nine stones named in Ezekiel 28:13. Dim: the three not listed.

Isaiah 14:12–15

“Day Star, son of Dawn”

Isaiah taunts the king of Babylon as Hêlēl ben Šāḥar, “Shining one, son of Dawn”: the planet Venus, brightest in the pre-dawn sky, climbing high and then vanishing as the sun rises. Latin translated it lucifer, “light-bearer”. Press through the five “I will”s.

    “But you are brought down to Sheol, to the far reaches of the pit.” Isa 14:15

    Two paths: grasping up, or stooping down

    The Day Star tries to climb to God’s throne and falls. Christ “did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself … to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted him” (Phil 2:6–9). He, not the rebel, is “the bright morning star” (Rev 22:16).

    Throne guardians

    Seraphim: the burning serpents

    In Isaiah 6 the beings around God’s throne are śərāphîm, usually “burning ones”. But śārāph is also a word for a serpent. A serpentine guardian at the throne was a familiar idea to ancient readers, which makes the nāḥāš in God’s garden less strange.

    Isaiah 6:2

    Above the throne

    “Above him stood the seraphim. Each had six wings: with two he covered his face, and with two he covered his feet, and with two he flew.”

    Numbers 21:6

    Fiery serpents

    “The LORD sent fiery serpents (hannəḥāšîm haśśərāphîm) among the people.” Both words, side by side.

    Isaiah 14:29; 30:6

    Flying seraph

    Isaiah twice mentions “a flying fiery serpent” (śārāph məʿôpēph): a winged serpent.

    Egypt

    The uraeus

    Egyptian royal and divine images were guarded by the rearing, often winged, cobra. It is the regional counterpart of the cherub, the hybrid guardian of Mesopotamia and Phoenicia.

    Genesis 3:14–15

    The curse, decoded

    Read as zoology, the curse misfires. Read as a fall in status, from God’s mountain to the dust of death, it makes sense, and it ends with a promise. Tap each line, then follow the serpent’s long way down through Scripture.

    Zeraʿ · “offspring, seed”

    The war of the two seeds

    Genesis 3:15 sets two lines against each other. The woman’s seed runs through Seth, Noah, Abraham, Judah and David to one person, while the serpent strikes again and again to cut it off. Press play, or choose any moment.

    The woman’s seedThe serpent strikes

    Numbers 21:4–9 · John 3:14–15

    Look and live

    In the wilderness God sends fiery serpents as judgment. Moses intercedes, and God tells him to make a serpent and set it on a pole: “if a serpent bit anyone, he would look at the bronze serpent and live.” It was a nəḥaš nəḥōšet, a shining serpent.

    These serpents are God’s instruments, not the rebel of Eden, so the two should not be merged. Yet Jesus takes up the image: “As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.” The symbol of death and deception becomes the sign of healing, because he was “made to be sin” for us (2 Cor 5:21).

    Centuries later Israel was burning incense to the bronze serpent, and King Hezekiah smashed it. He called it Nehushtan (2 Kgs 18:4), a pun on nāḥāš and nəḥōšet: “just a bronze snake thing.” A gift of healing had become an idol.

    Revelation 12:9 · 20:2

    “That ancient serpent”

    Revelation names him outright: “the great dragon … that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan, the deceiver of the whole world.” He is not a new character. He is the one from the garden, and the whole Bible traces his defeat.

    Psalm 74:13–14

    Leviathan’s heads crushed

    “You broke the heads of the sea monsters on the waters. You crushed the heads of Leviathan.” Chaos-serpent imagery, with heads crushed, as in Genesis 3:15.

    Isaiah 27:1

    The twisting serpent

    “In that day the LORD with his hard and great and strong sword will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent (nāḥāš), Leviathan the twisting serpent.”

    Hebrews 2:14 · 1 John 3:8

    Destroyed by the seed

    He shared our flesh “that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil.” “The reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the works of the devil.”

    “He shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel.” The first promise in the Bible is spoken to the serpent, inside his own curse.Genesis 3:15
